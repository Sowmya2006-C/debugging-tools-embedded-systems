Why debugging inside a chip is hard

Embedded systems

An embedded system is a computer built into a product to do one job: a car's brake controller, a thermostat, an insulin pump. It runs firmware on a microcontroller and talks to sensors and motors directly.

Debugging

Debugging is the disciplined search for why a system does something other than intended. On a PC you print to a console. On a chip you must halt the CPU, probe pins, or record signals.

Why it matters

A bug in a phone app is an annoyance. A bug in a pacemaker or flight controller is a safety event. Good tools shorten the search and prove the fix.

  • Limited visibility: no screen, few spare pins
  • Strict timing: stopping the CPU changes behaviour
  • Hardware and software faults look identical
  • Tiny memory leaves no room for logging
  • Faults that appear once a week

    Where these tools earn their keep

      Hard bugs and how to handle them

      Timing bugs
      Symptom: works under the debugger, fails at full speed. Capture with a logic analyzer or trace, never with breakpoints.
      Race conditions
      Interrupts and tasks touch shared data. Guard it with critical sections or atomics, and log event order with timestamps.
      Memory corruption
      Use watchpoints on suspect addresses, enable the MPU, add stack canaries and check stack depth.
      Hardware and software integration
      Prove the hardware first with a scope, then the firmware. Change one side at a time.
      Real-time limits
      Halting breaks deadlines. Use trace, GPIO toggles and cycle counters to measure without stopping.
      Intermittent faults
      Log to a ring buffer in RAM, trigger capture on the fault, and record supply voltage and temperature alongside.
      Low-power debugging
      Debug probes can keep clocks alive and hide sleep bugs. Measure current with a shunt and verify behaviour with the probe disconnected.
